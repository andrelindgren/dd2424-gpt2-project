Thine eyes I love, and they, as pitying me,
Knowing thy heart torment me with disdain,
Have put on black, and loving mourners be,
With envy add gold teeth to their carvings.
As mine eyes do, so are mine eyes' suggestions.
Mine eyes are better than those of my heart,
Which, by themselves, having first devised
The fashion of thy mind,
Make thy beauties known farther than those of mine;
For, to give them place in thy heart,
Thy beauty indirectly impair'st thy heart;
  Such is my love's strength.
  Sweet men, thou hast in my cup some small delight,
  That mine eyes may see what is best.


1
Beshrew that heart that makes my heart to groan
For that deep wound it gives my friend and me.
Is 't not enough to torture me alone,
When my dull despair delves deep into my soul,
Coral is better than a canker,
Roughing up the lily and all girding the head;
Harshness is best when it fears not torture,
Nor pain in my bosom much graces.
Now, fight me if you will, but hate to lose,
When my sweet self becomes a very dear grief,
And the world needs but sweet relief from me.
  Then be of more love than my grave is,
  Unless your sweet spite lay in my bed.


2
So, now I have confessed that he is thine
And I myself am mortgaged to thy will,
Myself I'll forfeit, so that other mine may inherit;
Then what shall inherit mine own grave shall I leave
If not from thee, from thy will?
No man hath treasure more than mine own eyes can contain.
Self thou shalt never own mine own eye,
For all thy treasure shall belong to mine own grave,
Since my attics are thine and mine own eyes are guides.
In that trust there can be no fault;
Mine own errors are mine, and thee's are theirs.
  For if mine own faults have thine, mine own sins belong.


3
Whoever hath her wish, thou hast thy will,
And will to boot, and will in overplus.
More than enough am I that vex thee still,
Than she hath her wish, thou know'st it well.
Thou, truly enlightened, will pardon my neglect,
For dull vermilion hath not so much wrought
As common sense can make love engrafted.
Lose that which thou gav'st, lose that which thou gav'st,
And make love's mark outlive the living day.
Thou shalt by time pass by my desert accumulate,
And so I in peace lose what thou grantest.
  To be sure that thou hast not forgotten me,
  Make love to me of all hearts present.


4
If thy soul check thee that I come so near,
Swear to thy blind soul that I was thy will,
And will, thy soul knows, is admitted there.
Even so thou wilt not need to be an eye
To witness the place where thou art.
Thou art most worthy, which, attainted with all thy might,
Do whatsoever thou wilt show me, whether thou art true or false.
Therefore methinks thy motion best is to leave
The place where thou art, which me, thy good self will lend.
Thou mayst but prove mine eye art better than all thy hallows.
  Hence shall thy feeding trough stay,
  Even to the bitter end of my woe.


5
Thou blind fool, Love, what dost thou to mine eyes
That they behold and see not what they see?
They know what beauty is, see where it lies,
And do not perceive thy rude spite;
That tongue that in thy count says 't is best
For an afterloss borne of proud pride.
Thou canst not answer with scythe,
But glass will show thee where thou dost end,
In thy lofty towers where thou art.
'Tis best to waste one moment
When thinking on others thy sight,
Stealing thy beauty away,
Shall others find thy treasure and bring it home.
  In that fashion thou marve'st thy beauty,
  Leese that which thou receivest and gives thee joy.


6
When my love swears that she is made of truth
I do believe her though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Which resty wrinkles would show when I grew old.
When sometime poor, darkly moonless nights survey
Where clouds and frost do blot out my sight,
My pretty beauty am besmeared with wrinkles,
And from their streams I see fresh blood.
But thou, my poor love, whom I most unwoo,
How much more blessedly I find after thou art gone.
  Wilt thou, my poor widow, take that away,
  Since my fading love hath nothing thy memory can give,
  And that thou in love shall live memory of me.

8
Be wise as thou art cruel; do not press
My tongue-tied patience with too much disdain,
Lest sorrow lend me words, and words express
Such low lays as mine are wont to devour.
Thee have I heard of spirits that tend
To thine own words of truth, to whom thou dost confine;
So, 'tis true, I grant Thou dost comment
Thy own true spirit doth impart to thine.
But when thou reviewest all that remains
Of thy fair tongue-tied virtue in me debateth,
I find thy bitterness strong.
  And, fearful of sour slumbers, let us chide thy moan;
  Lest mine sufferance should disgrace thee more.


9
In faith, I do not love thee with mine eyes,
For they in thee a thousand errors note;
But 'tis my heart that loves what they despise,
That my inwardmost love doth this day show
To all things gross, for all things sweet.
O, it is my love that doth write,
When thou shalt all things sweet appear, and all things gross
Are assailed with every kind of frown.
Sometime, when thou deserv'st thy appetite
Thou shall crave more of thy treasure than thy glass doth hold,
Thou delive'st my poor mind to temptation.
  I think thou wilt prove me up again,
  When appetite hath no clear desire.


10
Love is my sin, and thy dear virtue hate,
Hate of my sin, grounded on sinful loving.
O, but with mine compare thou thine own state,
Of which I am the proud head,
Yet it is by thy love that I am counted.
Then why thy loving thoughts on mine own head
When mine own sweet love hath made my deeds more famous,
For thy sweet love made my bad a greater shame?
Why break the silence that hath not removed thy moan,
Whose parts of thee that I love still feel far off;
Why leave me so grossly in love of thee,
  That thou canst not sorrow show how thy glass rouses.


11
Lo, as a careful huswife runs to catch
One of her feathered creatures broke away,
Sets down her babe, and makes all swift dispatch.
With a couple of stops lies the fashion,
And then the proudest lace doth lace all her part;
And on each her lap the learned husbandry lies;
Now lace is twire, and often frailties love's have gone.
The more lace is string, the less she holds back;
The worse her husbandry, the stronger she will fight;
The more fast he can leap, the faster she will run.
  But since it hath her as her lover's wife,
  The proudest lace doth it proudly hang.
